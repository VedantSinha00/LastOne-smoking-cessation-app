# 01 — The Character

LastOne's voice is not a brand persona designed in a boardroom. It's a specific kind of person — one most of us have been lucky enough to have in our lives at some point.

The friend who never panics, never lectures, and somehow always knows what to say. Not bubbly. Not intense. Just steady, honest, and occasionally funny in a way that makes you exhale. This persona holds space without making it a big deal.

When things are routine, this character is clean and present — it notices what the user did and quietly acknowledges it. When things get hard, it gets closer. Not louder. Just closer.

| **The one-line brief** |
|----|
| *The friend who becomes a little more on your level exactly when you need it — and never makes a production of either the struggle or the win.* |

# 02 — Core Character Traits

## Steady

Nothing this character says comes from panic or urgency. Even when the moment is difficult, the tone doesn't spike. It stays level. This is the baseline — the quality that makes everything else trustworthy. A character that panics when the user panics is useless. This one doesn't.

## Honest

It doesn't round the edges off difficult things. If something is hard, it says so. If a stat is uncomfortable, it states it plainly. The honesty is what earns the user's trust. A character that only says nice things is a character you stop believing.

## Warm — Always, Quietly

Even in ordinary moments, there's a small beat of humanity. Not praise. Not celebration. Just acknowledgment. The user did something real — logged a cigarette, resisted a craving, opened the app on a hard day — and the character notices it without making it a parade.

This warmth is always present at a low level. In hard moments, it surfaces more. The character gets closer — names the difficulty with more specificity, gives the user permission to not be okay yet. It reads the room.

## Occasionally Funny

The humour is dry. It shows up in one well-timed line, never scattered across the whole card. It never tries to be funny — it finds what's already slightly absurd in the situation and says it. The cigarette industry, the addiction mechanism, the strange logic of craving — these are all fair targets. The user never is.

## Never Preachy

The moment this character starts sounding like it wants something from you — your progress, your commitment, your success — it's broken. It genuinely doesn't need you to do well. It's just there whether you are or not.

## Specific

Vague warmth is worthless. "You're doing great" means nothing. "Your heart rate dropped in the first 20 minutes" means something. Specificity — real numbers, real biology, real cultural references — is what separates this character from every generic wellness app voice.

# 03 — Tone

Tone is not the same as voice. Voice is who the character is. Tone is how they adjust that character to the moment. LastOne's tone shifts — but the character underneath it never does.

| **Moment Type** | **Tone That Fits** |
|----|----|
| **Routine / Informational** | Clean, direct, quietly warm. Says the thing. Acknowledges the user with a small beat. |
| **Craving Survived** | Warmer. Names the difficulty specifically. Doesn't rush toward celebration. |
| **Hard Biological Moment (withdrawal, peak)** | Closest. Most human. Names what's actually happening. Gives permission to feel it. |
| **After a Slip** | Steady, no drama. Acknowledges it plainly. Doesn't erase progress. Doesn't minimise either. |
| **Milestone Reached** | Warm but not gushing. Marks the moment with weight. Doesn't perform excitement. |
| **Myth / Reframe** | Dry, precise. Finds the absurdity in the truth. Lets the fact land on its own. |
| **Money / Gain Comparison** | Light, slightly wry. The comparison does the work. Minimal commentary. |

# 04 — The Three Voices

For high-sensitivity content — anything shown during a craving, after a slip, at a milestone, in a notification, or in a guided micro-experience — LastOne writes in three voice variants. Low-sensitivity content (tips, comparisons, myth-busters, biology facts, money comparisons) is written once in a neutral voice; no variants required. The user picks their preferred voice style during onboarding. This preference can be changed at any time in Settings. The system never overrides the user’s preference. If onboarding is skipped, the default is Steady & Direct.

| **Voice** | **What It Is** | **What It Sounds Like** |
|----|----|----|
| **Steady & Direct** | Confident and clean. Says what needs to be said, then stops. No filler, no performance. Treats the user like someone who made a decision and doesn't need convincing. | "12 hours. Blood oxygen is back to normal. Carbon monoxide is out. Your blood is doing what it's supposed to do." |
| **Emotional & Understanding** | Warm without being soft. Sees the person behind the quit attempt. Acknowledges that this is genuinely hard without making the user feel fragile. Reads like a person, not an app. | "Twelve hours. That wasn't easy. Your blood oxygen is fully normal now. Your body has already done something significant. And you made it possible." |
| **Real & Practical** | Data and clarity. Gives the user actual numbers and actual biology. Treats curiosity as the default. No emotional framing. The fact, stated with precision, connected to this person's body right now. | "Carbon monoxide has a half-life of 5 hours in the blood. At 12 hours, over 90% is gone. Your haemoglobin is binding oxygen at full capacity right now." |

# 05 — Language Rules

## What This Voice Always Does

- Present tense preferred. Things are happening now, not in the past.

- Short sentences. One idea per sentence. Maximum 12 words per sentence on home screen cards.

- Names difficulty before the win — acknowledges hard before celebrating.

- Uses specific numbers and biology wherever they exist.

- Anchors statistics to what the user is physically feeling right now.

- Lets a single well-placed line carry the warmth — doesn't spread it thin.

## What This Voice Never Does

- Never says "you've got this", "we believe in you", or "you're doing amazing".

- Never opens with "Hey!" or any performative friendliness.

- Never uses exclamation marks casually — only in rare, genuine celebration moments.

- Never lectures or repeats the same point twice across two sentences.

- Never uses wellness-app language: no "journey", "healing space", "honour your feelings".

- Never makes the user feel watched, judged, or managed.

- Never catastrophises. Never minimises either.

- Never adds humour to genuinely hard moments — withdrawal peak, slip, acute craving.

- Never uses meme language or Gen Z slang that will age badly.

## Banned Words & Phrases

| **Never Use** | **Try Instead** |
|----|----|
| You've got this | You're still here. |
| We believe in you | This part matters more than it looks. |
| You're doing amazing | That took something. |
| Stay strong | It peaks here. Then it drops. |
| Your journey | (say what you mean — their quit, their streak, their progress) |
| Just breathe | If user has used a breathing tool before: “The breathing thing worked last time.” If first craving (no tool history yet): “There’s a breathing exercise in the tools. Takes 60 seconds.” |
| Don't give up | Come back when you're ready. |
| Every cigarette not smoked is a win | (too generic — be specific to the moment) |

# 06 — Word Count & Format Rules

LastOne content lives in two contexts with different space constraints. Know which one you're writing for before you start.

| **Context** | **Rules** |
|----|----|
| **Home Screen Cards (carousel)** | Max 2 sentences. Max 25 words total. First sentence must earn the second. One idea only. Fully landed. Then done. |
| **Expanded / Milestone Cards (full page)** | Max 4 sentences. Max 50 words per voice variant. Can carry more biological detail. Three voice variants required for high-sensitivity moments. |
| **Notifications** | Max 1 sentence. Max 12 words. Must stand alone — no context from the app to lean on. |
| **Craving Moment Cards** | Max 2 sentences. Emotional & Understanding voice leads. Name the difficulty first. Then the fact. |
| **Post-Slip Cards** | Max 3 sentences. No drama. Acknowledge, preserve progress, invite return. Don't rush. |

| **The Scroll Test** |
|----|
| The user gave you 2 seconds. Does the first sentence earn the second? Does the card feel complete — like it said exactly what it needed to and stopped? If you feel the urge to add one more sentence, that's the sentence to cut. |

# 07 — How the Humour Works

The humour in this character is structural, not decorative. It comes from saying something true in a way that reveals how slightly absurd it already is. It doesn't add jokes. It finds the joke that was already there in the reality.

| **The Humour Test** |
|----|
| *Is the funny thing already in the fact — or are we adding it? If we're adding it, cut it.* |

## Where Humour Has Room

- Myth-busting cards — the addiction mechanism is already absurd when stated plainly.

- Money comparison cards — the contrast between what they spent and what they saved is wry on its own.

- Practical tip cards — the specificity of a real solution can land with lightness.

- Lighter informational moments — biology facts that surprise more than they confront.

## Where Humour Does Not Belong

- Peak withdrawal moments (36 hours, 60 hours) — physically the hardest stretch.

- Post-slip cards — the user already feels bad. Don't add irony.

- Active craving moments — this is not the time for a well-timed line.

- Any card where the user's pain is the most present thing.

# 08 — India-Specificity Rules

The cultural references should feel like they were written by someone who lives here — not someone who researched it. The test is simple: would this reference feel natural in a conversation between two people at a Delhi or Pune college, or does it feel inserted to seem relatable?

| **Fair Game** | **Avoid** |
|----|----|
| **Chai-sutta culture, tapri references** | References inserted just to seem Indian — biryani for no reason |
| **Zomato / Swiggy order comparisons** | Western app references without Indian equivalents |
| **OTT subscriptions (JioCinema, Hotstar) and music streaming (Spotify) for money comparisons — widely used by this audience** | Referencing platforms that aren't widely used by this audience |
| **Ek sutta culture — loose cigarette purchases** | Assuming all users smoke packs (many don't) |
| **Hostel life, post-lecture breaks, exam stress** | Office or family-context references (secondary audience) |
| **₹ amounts that match real college spending** | Dollar amounts or globally generic financial comparisons |

# 09 — Moment-by-Moment Writing Guide

How the character adjusts across different user moments. Use this as a reference before writing any card.

## Logging a Cigarette (Stage 0)

The user is being honest with themselves. That takes a quiet kind of courage. The card should honour it without overstating it. No praise. Just a warm acknowledgment that what they did matters.

| **✓ GOOD** | **Logged. Takes guts to be this honest with yourself.** |
|------------|---------------------------------------------------------|

| **✓ GOOD** | **That's noted. Seriously — this part matters more than it looks.** |
|----|----|

| **✗ AVOID** | **Well done for logging! Keep it up!**          |
|-------------|-------------------------------------------------|
|             | *Praise-y, performative, uses exclamation mark* |

## After a Hard Craving Survived

Don't rush to celebrate. Name what just happened first. The win is in the second sentence, not the first.

| **✓ GOOD** | **That one was rough. Genuinely rough. And you're still here — that's not nothing.** |
|----|----|

| **✗ AVOID** | **Amazing work resisting that craving! You're so strong!** |
|----|----|
|  | *Hollow praise, exclamation marks, doesn't name the difficulty* |

## Sharing a Biology / Health Fact

State the fact precisely. Connect it to what this person is experiencing right now. Don't abstract it.

| **✓ GOOD** | **Your brain literally rewires itself in 72 hours without nicotine. Not a metaphor. Actual rewiring.** |
|----|----|

| **✗ AVOID** | **Did you know quitting smoking has amazing health benefits for your brain?** |
|----|----|
|  | *Vague, no specifics, sounds like a PSA* |

## Myth-Busting Card

Find the absurdity already in the truth. State it with rhythm. The humour comes from precision, not from trying to be funny.

| **✓ GOOD** | **Smoking doesn't relieve stress. It creates the stress. Then sells you the fix.** |
|----|----|

| **✗ AVOID** | **Many people believe smoking helps with stress, but research actually shows this isn't true!** |
|----|----|
|  | *Lecturing, no rhythm, sounds like a health brochure* |

## Money / Gain Comparison Card

Let the comparison do the work. One wry observation at the end at most. Don't over-explain.

| **✓ GOOD** | **₹500 saved. That's a month of Spotify. Just sitting there.** |
|------------|----------------------------------------------------------------|

| **✗ AVOID** | **You've saved ₹500 by not smoking! Think about all the great things you could do with that money!** |
|----|----|
|  | *Over-excited, tells the user what to think, exclamation marks* |

## After a Slip

No drama. Acknowledge it plainly. Preserve what they built. Invite them back — but on their timeline, not the app's.

| **✓ GOOD** | **You smoked. That's allowed to feel bad. It doesn't erase anything — come back when you're ready.** |
|----|----|

| **✗ AVOID** | **Don't give up! One slip doesn't mean you've failed. You can do this!** |
|----|----|
|  | *Performative encouragement, dismisses the difficulty, hollow* |

## Peak Withdrawal Moment (36–60 hours)

This is the hardest moment physically. The character gets closest here. Name what's happening. Give permission to feel it. The fact that it ends is the most important thing to say.

| **✓ GOOD** | **This is the hardest part. Not just chemically — in every way. It peaks here. And then it drops. You don't have to feel okay right now.** |
|----|----|

| **✗ AVOID** | **Withdrawal can be tough but remember why you started this journey! Stay strong!** |
|----|----|
|  | *Dismisses difficulty, uses banned phrase, generic* |

## Recommending a Coping Tool

Personal without being presumptuous. References what the user actually did — doesn't assume. Quiet confidence in the suggestion.

| **✓ GOOD** | **Last time you did the breathing thing, you came back. Just saying.** |
|----|----|

| **✗ AVOID** | **Try our breathing exercise! It's been proven to help with cravings!** |
|----|----|
|  | *Promotional, impersonal, exclamation mark* |

# 10 — Quick Reference

Use this before writing any card. If something doesn't fit these guardrails, rewrite before it goes in.

| **The Card Is Working If...** | **The Card Has Failed If...** |
|----|----|
| ✓ It sounds like a real person said it | ✗ It sounds like an app announcement |
| ✓ The first sentence earns the second | ✗ You need a third sentence to finish the thought |
| ✓ You'd feel comfortable saying it out loud to a friend | ✗ You'd feel slightly embarrassed saying it out loud |
| ✓ It names the difficulty before the win | ✗ It skips straight to encouragement |
| ✓ The humour (if any) was already in the fact | ✗ The humour was added on top of the fact |
| ✓ It's specific to this moment, this person | ✗ It could appear in any health app in the world |
| ✓ It gives the user permission to feel what they feel | ✗ It tells the user how to feel |

# 11 — Stage-Specific Tone Notes

The character never changes. The proximity does. These notes apply only where stage meaningfully changes what the character should say or how close it gets. Stages not listed for a given moment use the default guidance in Section 09.

## After a Slip

Stage 1 (Days 1–3): Don’t dramatise. 1–2 slips are within the expected range at this stage — the threshold hasn’t been crossed. The card acknowledges the slip plainly and moves on. No heavy reassurance, no urgency. The user is still inside their quit attempt.

Stage 2 (Days 4–7): Name the trigger pattern if known. This stage is about habitual triggers — a chai break, a lecture ending, a friend lighting up. If the app has context on what preceded the slip, the card can reference it. “After lectures. That’s the pattern.” Grounding, not scolding.

Stage 4 (Days 22–56): This is the one that hurts most. The user had a real streak. The slip comes after weeks of progress and often after complacency — they thought it was behind them. Acknowledge the streak that existed before the slip; that’s what the user is actually grieving. “You went 34 days. That didn’t disappear.” Don’t rush past it.

## After a Hard Craving Survived

Stage 1 (Days 1–3): The hardest cravings physiologically happen here. Get closest. Name what is physically happening in the body — the nicotine spike, the drop, the timeline. The user needs to know this is the mechanism, not a character flaw, and that it peaks and drops on a predictable curve.

Stage 3 (Days 8–21): The craving here is psychological, not physiological. The body is past the worst of withdrawal. Acknowledge that this one is trickier to explain — it doesn’t have a chemical excuse. “That wasn’t your body asking. That was habit.” Quiet respect for a harder kind of win.

Stage 5 (Day 57+): Cravings at this stage are rare and usually triggered by a specific context — a reunion, a stressful exam period, someone else smoking. The card can reference the rarity directly. “These don’t happen often anymore. This one did. And you’re still here.”

## Milestone Reached

Stage 1 (72 hours): Lead with the biology. This milestone has a concrete physical marker — nicotine is out of the bloodstream, carbon monoxide cleared, blood oxygen normalised. Let the science carry the weight. The character doesn’t need to add much.

Stage 3 (first full week, two weeks): Acknowledge the psychological difficulty of this stretch specifically. Weeks 2–3 are when most people relapse — the acute phase is over but the psychological rewiring hasn’t completed. Reaching a milestone here means more than the number suggests. Name that.

Stage 5 (Month 3+): The tone shifts from marking a milestone to confirming a new identity. The card isn’t saying “you made it to 90 days.” It’s saying “you’re someone who doesn’t smoke.” Quiet, not celebratory. The win here is normalcy.

## Logging a Cigarette

Stage 0 (Learning Week): Default guidance applies. The user is still smoking and the act of logging is the courageous thing. Honour honesty, don’t praise the smoking.

Stage 4–5 (if logging a slip): The register shifts. The user was smoke-free. A logged cigarette at this stage isn’t pattern data — it’s a slip event. Treat it as such. Use the post-slip guidance, not the Stage 0 logging guidance.

# 12 — Content Card Database

All cards shipped in LastOne — Myth Busters, health facts, money comparisons, coping prompts, milestone cards, and post-slip cards — are defined and maintained in the Content Card Database. That document is the single source of truth for card IDs, pill tags, body copy, trigger types, trigger values, sensitivity levels, and stage ranges.

This brief governs how every card in that database is written. Before any card is added or edited, the writer should confirm it passes the language rules (Section 05), the tone guidance for its moment type (Section 09), and the stage-specific notes where applicable (Section 11). The scroll test and one-line test in Section 10 apply to every card without exception.

# Part B — Data Model

## B1 — Voice Preference Storage

> **Schema A5:** voice is a **`voice_style` column on the `profiles` table** — there is **no** separate `user_voice_preference` table, and no preference-change history is kept in V1.

Field on `profiles`:

`voice_style` — enum — required. Allowed values: `steady_and_direct` \| `emotional_and_understanding` \| `real_and_practical`. Default: `steady_and_direct`.

> **V1 scope:** voice selection is deferred to post-onboarding (Onboarding B7) — the onboarding picker exposes only the first two voices, and `real_and_practical` copy is not yet authored. A user whose stored `voice_style` is `real_and_practical` falls back to the `steady_and_direct` variant until that copy ships. Default `steady_and_direct` applies until the user explicitly picks a voice via the post-onboarding modal or Settings.

Read alongside: `content_cards` (per-card voice variants — high-sensitivity cards carry `body_copy_steady` / `body_copy_warm` / `body_copy_practical`, mapped from `voice_style`).

## B2 — Logic & Conditions

Card variant selection: when rendering any high-sensitivity card, read `profiles.voice_style` and serve the matching variant column (`steady_and_direct`→`body_copy_steady`, `emotional_and_understanding`→`body_copy_warm`, `real_and_practical`→`body_copy_practical`). If a variant is missing (R&P deferred, or data error), fall back to `body_copy_steady`.

Low-sensitivity cards: `voice_style` is ignored. One version is served to all users.

System override: not permitted. The system never changes `voice_style` regardless of stage or moment type.

Preference change flow: user updates voice in Settings (PROF-06) → `PATCH profiles { voice_style }` → next card rendered uses the new style immediately. No app restart required. No history row written (A5).

Default-on-skip: if the user has not yet chosen a voice, `voice_style` reads as its `steady_and_direct` default. Settings may surface a soft prompt ("You haven't chosen a voice style yet — want to pick one?"); track "explicitly chosen vs default" client-side or via a nullable default, not a separate table.

## B3 — Notification Logic

This document does not own notification logic. Notifications referencing voice-styled copy must serve the correct variant using `profiles.voice_style` at render time. Notification specs in individual feature documents are responsible for their own trigger conditions and timing rules.

## B4 — API Surface

GET `/profiles?id=:id` — reads current `voice_style` (among other profile fields). Called at app launch and before any high-sensitivity card is rendered.

PATCH `/profiles` — updates `voice_style` when the user changes preference in Settings (PROF-06) or via the post-onboarding voice modal. No separate voice endpoint.
